## Case A: capable operations within a changing context

The report describes technical guidance, humour, structured content and active moderation. The author interprets the guidance as meeting beginners' desire to improve; this need was not independently validated. My moderation experience provides first-hand access to how order and questions were managed. These observations complicate an explanation based solely on poor operation. The report also describes A using game-balance debates and unconventional challenges as topical content; it does not quantify their effect on discovery.

The author attributes withdrawal to a combination of progression costs, reduced technical barriers through automation, a shift towards socially demanding competitive play, a declining category and personal pressures. Those are explanatory claims in the report. The chronology of game changes remains unverified, and their relative contribution cannot be separated from the creator's circumstances. [A-01](EVIDENCE_MAP.md#a-01-teaching-and-moderation), [A-02](EVIDENCE_MAP.md#a-02-withdrawal-and-context)

## Case B: performance, presence and selective recognition

B's reported offer extended beyond skilled play. Regular broadcasting provided a recurring presence, while public interaction remained limited. More personalised attention appeared in supporter contact and prioritised play. Membership levels and moderator status also offered visible recognition, with some status holders volunteering community work. [B-01–B-05](EVIDENCE_MAP.md#b-01-presentation-and-distribution)

The report also describes reusing successful content and maintaining short updates. It interprets B's scheduling as avoiding competition with larger creators; neither an acquisition-cost advantage nor a scheduling effect was measured.

The report interprets this combination as a companionship-oriented offer, with gifting connected to attention and status. It does not establish that every supporter sought the same experience, that the presentation was fully strategic or that this model could be replicated elsewhere.

## What the contrast supports

**Interpretation:** community tactics depend on what value is available and meaningful in context. A moderation role cannot be understood from its label alone; frequent public chat is not the only possible expression of engagement; technical skill is only one candidate explanation of payment.

**Hypothesis:** a tactic that works where audiences value companionship and personalised play may transfer poorly to a community organised around different progression and social demands. This preserves the report's category-fit argument without claiming that category predetermines outcomes.

The report also contrasts video-led discovery with a more established livestream ecosystem and suggests links between game-character choice and paying audiences. These are wider contextual interpretations requiring separate evidence, not established genre-wide facts.
